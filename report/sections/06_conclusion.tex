\section{Conclusion}

Based on questionnaire data from 32 female college students and 3 semi-structured interviews, this study found that resistance training avoidance among female college students in campus gyms is more primarily a spatial and beginner barrier issue rather than solely an appearance internalization issue.
Participants were not lacking in exercise interest---the majority had used the campus gym and maintained a regular exercise frequency---but utilization of the free-weight area was notably low, with 68.75\% actively avoiding it at least ``sometimes.''
The most prominent barriers were the male-dominated environment, unfamiliarity with equipment, not knowing where to begin, and the discomfort caused by open, visible spaces; ``do not want to build visible muscle'' was selected by 0\% of participants as an avoidance reason.
Training self-efficacy showed virtually no association with avoidance ($\rho = -.11$), further indicating that the barrier is situational rather than dispositional.

The central conclusion is that in the present sample's campus context, female students' negotiation centers primarily on spatial conditions rather than on appearance norms.
Participants sought strategic ways to participate---off-peak timing, companion accompaniment, area switching---rather than making trade-offs between health and appearance.
This ``negotiated participation'' echoes \textcite{xiong2021fanshen}'s observation that Chinese women's fitness practices are neither fully disciplined nor fully empowered, but involve continuous adjustment of body, space, and self-identity.

In practice, the findings suggest that universities could prioritize beginner-friendly women's strength training workshops (supported by 96.88\% of participants in the upper score range), semi-private spatial modifications, and equipment instruction.
Participants who avoided the free-weight area more frequently were also more supportive of such interventions ($\rho = .45$, $p = .009$), indicating that the strongest demand for change comes from those most affected by the barriers.

Theoretically, the integrated three-layer framework received asymmetric support: spatial production had the strongest explanatory power, appearance internalization was weaker than anticipated, and behavioral outcomes manifested as strategic negotiation rather than binary avoidance.
This asymmetry itself is a finding---it suggests that among female college students lacking systematic strength training experience, spatial and beginner barriers may be more proximal obstacles than appearance internalization, and that different barrier factors may vary in priority depending on group characteristics.

Future research should expand the sample to multiple universities with larger samples, preserve participant-level raw response data, and employ fully validated scales.
On the qualitative side, increasing the number of interviewees and conducting member checking would enhance trustworthiness.
If conditions permit, a pre--post comparison design could directly evaluate whether beginner-friendly workshops and spatial interventions reduce resistance training avoidance.
Such research could also examine whether the asymmetry observed here shifts in samples with more training experience, where appearance internalization may play a relatively larger role as spatial familiarity increases.

Given the limited sample size of this study and the fact that all individual-level statistics are exploratory bivariate analyses, the conclusions should not be extrapolated as causal laws; however, the findings clearly suggest that gender equity in campus gyms depends not only on equipment availability, but also on how spaces are experienced, who is able to make mistakes and learn within them, and whether female beginners can be seen as legitimate users.
